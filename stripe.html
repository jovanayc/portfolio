PRODUCT STRATEGY CASE STUDY

Trustworthy People Team answers for employees, and a private path when it matters

I put this together to practice thinking about Stripe's People products the way I would on the Employee Experiences team. I used public research, Stripe's public job postings, three real interviews, and simulated personas, which I label clearly as hypotheses.

ForProduct Manager, Employee Experiences
AuthorJovanay Carter
ProductInternal People tools: benefits and leave
DescriptionOutside case study

My understanding of Stripe's Employee Experiences team

This team builds internal products for Stripe's own employees, managers, and People professionals. The goal is to make routine questions and tasks self-service, while making sure anything that needs judgment or care reaches the right person with the right context.

Based on Stripe's public job postings, People information lives across a few separate places: Workday for employee records, an internal wiki for policies, and a ticketing system for HR questions. This case study focuses on the moment an employee has a benefits or leave question and has to figure out where to go.

01Why I started here

I started my career in technical recruiting at Google and Codecademy, so I've been on the People side of these questions. Later I built Buddy at The Dev Difference, an AI interviewer where trust and human decisions were central to the product. This role sits right where those two experiences meet.

The constraint I kept in mind is trust, not speed. Security, legal, and Employee Relations rightly move carefully with employee data, so anything I proposed had to earn trust first and scale second.

02Research signals

I pulled from SHRM, Gartner, Korn Ferry, and several workplace surveys. I interviewed three people: a claims specialist at a healthcare billing company in Chicago, a marketing operations manager at an IT company in Germany, and a battalion chief who manages about 250 people at a public-sector fire department. I also wrote 11 simulated employee personas to form hypotheses. I put each signal on a sticky note, color-coded by source, and sorted until patterns showed up.

The clearest pattern was how people look for help:

  • Checks online first: looks it up, and only contacts a person if that fails. They need answers they can trust are current.
  • Goes straight to a person: emails or files a ticket right away. They need to reach the right person without being bounced between teams.
  • Holds back or goes outside: avoids asking HR because they're worried who will find out. They need a private way to ask.

The secondary research backed this up. Employees resolve only about half of their HR needs on their own, and only 42% trust their employer to use AI in a way that benefits them.

My real interviews pointed in different directions. The claims specialist found HR on the company website and got what she needed, though she wants medical details seen only by whoever needs them. The marketing operations manager in Germany needed her salary and bonus explained after a merger, and spent about a week going from her VP to HR to another team. On anything sensitive, she was blunt:

“Even if you say it’s private, it’s not private.”

Her magic wand was more transparency about how things work inside the company.

The battalion chief mostly contacts HR about his people: personnel records, payroll checks, and leave cases. He emails on purpose, so there's a record. All three drew the same line for AI: facts, rules, and simple lookups are fine, but a person should handle anything specific to their situation. As the chief put it, “people have people problems.”

Because my real sample is small, I treat the simulated findings as directional until I can test them with more people.

That led me to one problem: Employees can't easily get trustworthy answers to benefits and leave questions, or privately reach the right person when the topic is sensitive.

03Creating a framework to narrow solutions

With the problem clear, I scored five possible solutions on three lenses: employee trust, People team workload, and fit with Stripe's goals. Trust gets the most weight, because if employees don't trust the answers, nothing else matters. Every candidate also had to pass a risk check: a reversible pilot, measurable within a quarter, and backed by more than one source.

The rejected options mattered as much as the winner. A general HR chatbot was too broad to test well, and AI-drafted performance reviews would save time but hurt trust.

That pointed to one clear direction: a benefits and leave assistant for routine questions, with a direct line to a People lead for anything sensitive.

04What I got wrong at first

I assumed privacy only mattered for the most serious cases, like reporting harassment. The research suggested it's much broader. People held back on asking about an internal move, a pregnancy they hadn't announced, or medical leave, because they didn't know who would see the question.

My interview in Germany added a second lesson:

Interviews revealed that in private situations, employees may distrust not just AI but any written communication.

So I stopped treating privacy as an edge case and started treating it as a reason people never ask at all. That changed the product from "a better answer engine" to "a place people feel safe asking."

I also guessed that written records would feel protective to managers and risky to employees. The interviews didn't support that. The claims specialist, an individual contributor, feels protected by records because she trusts the rules. What mattered was trust, not role.

05The direction I chose

I combined two ideas: a chat resource with policy access for quick questions, and private, direct conversations with team experts for anything sensitive.

06How much the AI decides

The core product question is how much the AI should do on its own. I placed each type of request on a level, based on how sensitive it is and whether it can be undone:

LevelExample
Level 1
AI answers, with the source		"What's my dental deductible?"
Level 2
AI acts after you confirm		"Add my newborn to my plan"
Level 3
AI recommends, then offers a person		"Should I take family leave or short-term disability?"
Level 4
AI hands off to a person, with context		"I need leave for a family medical crisis"
Level 5
Straight to a person, no AI		Reporting harassment or a manager's misconduct

At Level 5, the product should be honest about what happens next. Once HR knows about some issues, the company may be required to act, so it shouldn't promise more confidentiality than it can keep. Legal and Employee Relations would own that wording.

Privacy also showed up as two different needs. Some people trust HR but want sensitive details limited to whoever needs them, which calls for role-based access: employees, managers, and People partners each see only what their role requires. Others distrust HR itself, which a direct line to a People lead doesn't fully solve, so I'd test whether an option outside the usual HR route is needed. Managers, on the other hand, get a clear record of what they asked and what HR said.

07Mocking it up

Using the resources I had, I worked with Claude and a public example of Stripe's product style to create the look and layout of a first screen. Then I let my interviews decide what the screen actually shows and includes.

As I designed, I kept pulling the product back toward how people already get help:

  • Calendar, policies, and pay stay one click away. The claims specialist likes to look things up herself instead of asking AI every time.
  • Private needs go straight to a named person. I started designing a new request form, then realized people already email or call. So the screen just shows their People lead's email and phone, and when to expect a reply.
  • The AI explains the policy; a person confirms. The answer says what the policy allows instead of telling someone they're eligible.
  • Pay is a link, not an AI feature yet. Explaining pay and bonuses matters, as my Germany interview showed, but it's a later phase.

08A realistic 3-month roadmap

Month 1: validate the problem and set the scope. I would interview employees and People partners, map today's internal tools, and agree on boundaries with engineering, security, and legal. We would then build a test set of real benefits and leave questions with correct answers.

Month 2: build and test the first version. We would build policy chat for benefits and leave in one country, and show each employee their People lead's contact details. We would test answers against the question set before anyone uses it.

Month 3: pilot and refine. We would launch to a small group, review answers and feedback weekly, and decide whether to expand. Pay explanations and a manager view would come later.

09Outcomes I would measure

North star metricNew employee tickets who rarely spoke to HR before.Employees who had never brought a question to HR and now use the chat or reach out to their People lead. That's the clearest sign we created a safe place to get information.

Supporting signals:

  • HR tickets decreasing, and which topics they're about
  • Chat usage over time
  • How often people ask the chat for things they could easily look up, like their time off balance, so we can point them to the page instead and keep costs down
  • Employee satisfaction with the tool

10What I'd do with more information

With more information, I would:

  • Understand Stripe's current internal tooling and how employees use it today.
  • Interview employees internally about what works and what doesn't, and get clear boundaries from engineering teams.
  • Make sure this doesn't overlap with other products. If Stripe already has a pay tool, I wouldn't add pay here. I'd point people to that tool instead.
